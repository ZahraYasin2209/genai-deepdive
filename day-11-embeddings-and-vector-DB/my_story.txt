STORY: THE GLASS PLATE OF ANARKALI

LOCATION: ANARKALI BAZAAR, LAHORE

DATE: FEBRUARY 6, 2026

In the bustling heart of Anarkali Bazaar, where the scent of spiced nihari meets the colorful displays of unstitched lawn, lived an old shopkeeper named Sultan Ahmad. Sultan’s shop was a narrow room filled with photostat editions of 19th-century texts, specifically the 'Tahqiqat-e Chishti' (Chishti's Researches) compiled by Maulvi Nur Ahmad in 1860.

One foggy winter morning, a student named Asif arrived from Bahria Town. He wasn't looking for jewelry; he was searching for the "Glass Plate of the Delhi Gate." According to urban legend, during the construction of the Lahore Railway Junction in 1860, the British engineers had accidentally unearthed a glass plate containing a map of the hidden tunnels connecting the Walled City to the Shalimar Bagh.

Sultan Ahmad smiled and handed Asif a small, crumpled rupee note with a telephone number scribbled on it. "Visit Chacha Rafeeq at the Gawalmandi tea stall," he whispered. "He knows why the British headquarters were built over that specific track."

Asif’s journey took him from the grand arches of the Badshahi Masjid to the crowded platforms of the Lahore Junction. He discovered that the railway was not just for transport; it was a shield. The tracks were laid directly over the ancient 'Budha Ravi' (Old Ravi) riverbed to prevent the city's enemies from using the underground aqueducts.

The conflict began when a private developer, 'Indus-Heights,' started digging for a luxury hotel near the Mahmood Booti Embankment. They didn't care about the 3,700-year-old history of the 'Battle of the Ten Kings' fought on those very banks. If they hit the Budha Ravi line, the salt-water from the deeper earth would flood the foundations of the historic Walled City, turning Lahore’s heritage into a watery grave.
